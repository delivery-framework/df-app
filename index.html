Why Fusion

Built in days. Changed in hours.

Not a tidier process — a different set of numbers. How long it takes to get working software. How much of your people's week it costs to get it. What your auditors find when they look. And whether any of that still holds in three years, when most delivery has quietly slowed to a crawl.

Hours, not months of workshops

Fusion asks less of your business stakeholders. The sessions they sit in are captured, and the documents get written from what was actually said — not from a second round of workshops held to confirm the first. Nobody is pulled off their day job to explain the same thing to a new person.

Risk and compliance is built in

Bring the obligations from wherever they come — a contract, a regulator, a standard, your own policy. Each becomes a control, and each control is answered against the code as it actually is: the file, the dependency, the version. An obligation is met only when the evidence says so, and where it is not, the gap and what closes it sit beside it. The audit trail is a by-product of how the work runs, not a scramble before every release.

Idea to impact in days or weeks

Not months or years. One connected pipeline takes out the handoffs, the rework and the waiting that stretch enterprise delivery out.

User feedback live in hours

Feedback is collected continuously, not at the end of a phase. A change can be in production the same day — with requirements, tests and release evidence already up to date.

Agility that lasts, because the engineering does

Agile promised sustained agility and mostly delivered ceremony: the standups outlived the design reviews and the tests. Fusion puts the engineering back at the centre — the domain model, the workflow specs and the system tests are steps in the pipeline, not the first things dropped when a date gets tight. Which is why the hundredth change costs about what the first one did, instead of the decay every team knows: quick in year one, treacle by year three.

Significantly less code

The safest code is the code nobody has to write.

A business application is mostly the same screen over and over: a list, a record, a form, a permission check. Teams write those by hand, and lately they let an AI generate them — thousands of near-identical lines that nobody reads closely enough to catch the one that is wrong. Fusion does not produce those lines at all. It reads what your business objects already declare, and works the rest out from there.

Fusion does not ask you to choose between the two, and it does not make you start again to get from one to the other.

No lock-in

Here is exactly what it would cost you to leave.

Every platform claims there is no lock-in. Almost none of them will tell you what walking away actually involves. So here is the honest ledger, before you sign anything rather than after.

You keep

  • Your documents

    The discovery, the design decisions, the delivery record. They are plain files sitting in your repository, and they stay readable with or without us.

  • Your business logic

    The models and the rules that describe how your organisation actually works. That is the part that took the thinking, and it is ordinary Rails that any Rails developer can pick up.

  • Your database, and everything in it

    A standard PostgreSQL database with a standard schema. Not a file export you have to reassemble — the live thing, running, with your data in it.

You would have to replace

  • The interface

    Fusion generates your screens. Take Fusion out and the screens go with it, so you would build your own. This is the real cost of leaving and we are not going to pretend it is small.

  • The AI-assisted pipeline

    The path from discovery to delivery, and the conventions that keep AI output as reviewable work. You would go back to whatever you were doing before.

That is a viable escape hatch, not a full system replacement. You would be rebuilding an interface, not rebuilding a system.

Now weigh it against leaving a rented platform

Your data comes out as a file export that somebody has to reassemble into a working system. Your business rules were written in a language that runs nowhere else. Your screens were configuration inside a product you no longer have access to. There is no escape hatch, viable or otherwise — and that is not an oversight in the design. It is the commercial model.
